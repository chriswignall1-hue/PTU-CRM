# "On This Day" — Research Notes for Pure Transfer

**Purpose:** Historically accurate, dated events relevant to high‑purity fluid transfer, hygienic
process engineering, pharmaceutical/biotech/food/chemical/semiconductor manufacturing, materials,
reliability and quality. Raw material for "On This Day" LinkedIn content — you handle the styling,
tone and branding.

**How this is organised**
- **Part 1 — Dated events:** anchored to a specific calendar date (day + month). Use these as true
  "On This Day" posts. Chronological, January → December.
- **Part 2 — Year‑based milestones:** genuinely important developments where a single reliable
  calendar day doesn't exist (standards bodies, techniques that evolved over time). Slot these onto
  any relevant day, or use as evergreen posts. Year is accurate; I have deliberately **not**
  invented a day.

**Accuracy notes**
- Dates in Part 1 are either verified against sources during research or are very well established.
- A handful carry a flag where the *traditional* date is commonly cited but historically debated
  (e.g. Fleming). These are marked ⚠ — worth a final check before publishing that specific one.
- Nothing here is fabricated. Where I only knew the year, it's in Part 2, not dressed up with a fake day.

## Part 1 — Dated Events (Jan → Dec)

Each entry: **date — event (year)** · what happened · *why it's relevant to Pure Transfer's world.*

### January

**5 January — James Watt patents the separate condenser (1769)**
Watt's patent for "a new method of lessening the consumption of steam and fuel in fire engines"
introduced the separate condenser, roughly doubling steam‑engine efficiency and helping power the
Industrial Revolution.
*Relevance:* the foundation of industrial steam — the same thermodynamics behind steam sterilisation
(SIP), clean steam and process heating in modern hygienic plants.

**10 January — de Havilland Comet G‑ALYP breaks up in flight (1954)**
The world's first commercial jet airliner disintegrated over the Mediterranean near Elba. Investigation
traced it to metal fatigue cracking at the corners of near‑square window/hatch cutouts, driven by
repeated cabin pressurisation cycles.
*Relevance:* fatigue from pressure cycling, stress concentration at corners, and the value of full
root‑cause investigation — directly applicable to pressure vessels, piping and repeated CIP/SIP cycles.

**11 January — First successful clinical use of insulin (1922)**
14‑year‑old Leonard Thompson became the first person successfully treated with insulin, at Toronto
General Hospital, following Banting and Best's work. It turned a fatal diagnosis into a manageable
condition.
*Relevance:* one of the origin points of sterile injectable biologics manufacturing — and a
straight line to today's recombinant and biopharmaceutical production.

**15 January — Great Boston Molasses Flood (1919)**
A large storage tank holding ~2.3 million US gallons of molasses ruptured in Boston's North End,
releasing a wave that killed 21 people. The tank had been poorly built, never properly tested, and
showed warning leaks that were painted over rather than fixed.
*Relevance:* storage‑vessel integrity, material selection, and the danger of treating symptoms
(painting over leaks) instead of causes — prevention over reaction, in its bluntest form.

**16 January — Liberty ship SS Schenectady fractures in half (1943)**
Barely a day after sea trials, the tanker split almost completely in two while lying quietly at its
fitting‑out dock. The cause was brittle fracture in welded steel that lost toughness in cold
conditions, with cracks initiating at stress concentrations.
*Relevance:* brittle fracture, weld quality, notch toughness and low‑temperature behaviour of steels —
core materials‑integrity lessons for anyone building welded pressure‑containing equipment.

**27 January — Apollo 1 fire (1967)**
A flash fire during a ground test killed astronauts Grissom, White and Chaffee. A pure‑oxygen cabin
atmosphere, flammable materials and an inward‑opening hatch turned a small electrical fault into a
fatal fire.
*Relevance:* materials and atmosphere control, design review, and how "minor" test conditions can be
lethal — the discipline behind rigorous design and hazard review.

**28 January — Space Shuttle Challenger disaster (1986)**
Challenger broke apart 73 seconds after launch, killing all seven crew. The cause was a rubber O‑ring
seal in a solid rocket booster that lost elasticity in unusually cold weather, allowing hot gas to
escape. Engineers had raised concerns the night before.
*Relevance:* the classic seal‑failure and "normalisation of deviance" case study — elastomer
performance at temperature, the limits of a single seal, and listening to engineers who raise a flag.

### February

**1 February — Space Shuttle Columbia disaster (2003)**
Columbia disintegrated during re‑entry, killing all seven crew. A piece of foam insulation had struck
and breached the wing's thermal protection during launch; the damage went unresolved and hot gas
entered the wing structure on return.
*Relevance:* foreign‑object damage, the cost of not fully investigating a known anomaly, and the
importance of inspection and margins.

**11 February — Thomas Edison born (1847)**
Edison built one of the first industrial research laboratories (Menlo Park) and pioneered systematic,
team‑based R&D — the "invention factory" model.
*Relevance:* the birth of organised, methodical engineering development — the mindset behind
disciplined design and testing.

**23 February — ISO begins operations (1947)**
The International Organization for Standardization officially started work, following a 1946 meeting of
delegates from 25 countries in London. It now underpins thousands of standards used every day.
*Relevance:* the backbone of global standards — ISO 9001 (quality), ISO 14644 (cleanrooms), and the
shared language that lets high‑purity equipment be specified and trusted across borders.

**25 February — Patriot missile failure at Dhahran (1991)**
A Patriot battery failed to intercept an incoming Scud missile; 28 people were killed. The cause was a
tiny floating‑point rounding error in the system's clock that accumulated over ~100 hours of continuous
operation, throwing off the tracking calculation.
*Relevance:* small errors that compound over time — a reminder that reliability includes drift,
calibration and the assumptions buried in software and instrumentation.

### March

**11 March — Fukushima Daiichi nuclear accident (2011)**
A magnitude‑9 earthquake and tsunami knocked out power and backup cooling at the plant, leading to
core meltdowns. The flooding exceeded the design‑basis assumptions for sea‑wall height and backup
generator placement.
*Relevance:* beyond‑design‑basis events, defence‑in‑depth, and the danger of correlated failures
(one event taking out both primary and backup systems).

**20 March — Grover Shoe Factory boiler explosion (1905)**
A boiler explosion and subsequent fire and collapse in Brockton, Massachusetts killed 58 people. Public
outrage over repeated boiler disasters helped drive the creation of formal boiler codes.
*Relevance:* a direct ancestor of the ASME Boiler & Pressure Vessel Code — proof that pressure‑vessel
standards exist because people died before them.

**23 March — BP Texas City refinery explosion (2005)**
An explosion during the restart of an isomerisation unit killed 15 workers and injured many more.
Overfilling and overheating sent flammable liquid out of a blowdown stack; investigators cited cost
cutting, worn equipment and a weak safety culture.
*Relevance:* process safety, the difference between personal safety and process safety, and how
degraded equipment plus schedule pressure become catastrophe. (The Baker Panel report is a classic.)

**24 March — Robert Koch announces the tuberculosis bacterium (1882)**
Koch presented his discovery of *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, cementing germ theory and his rigorous
methods (Koch's postulates) for linking a microbe to a disease.
*Relevance:* the scientific foundation of contamination control, sterility and aseptic technique —
the reason we design out bioburden rather than hope it away.

**28 March — Three Mile Island partial meltdown (1979)**
A partial core meltdown at the Pennsylvania nuclear plant was driven by a stuck‑open valve plus
confusing, poorly designed instrumentation that led operators to make the wrong calls.
*Relevance:* human factors and control‑room/instrumentation design — reliable systems have to be
readable and understandable, not just technically correct.

### April

**6 April — Roy Plunkett discovers PTFE / Teflon (1938)**
At DuPont's Jackson Laboratory, Plunkett found that a cylinder of tetrafluoroethylene had spontaneously
polymerised into a slippery, chemically inert white solid. Its first major use was sealing valves and
piping on the Manhattan Project because it resisted highly corrosive process streams.
*Relevance:* PTFE and fluoropolymers are everywhere in high‑purity transfer — seals, gaskets,
diaphragms, linings and tubing where chemical inertness and cleanliness are non‑negotiable.

**13 April — Apollo 13 oxygen tank explosion (1970)**
An oxygen tank ruptured en route to the Moon ("Houston, we've had a problem"). A latent wiring/
thermostat defect, missed in testing, crippled the spacecraft — but disciplined engineering brought the
crew home.
*Relevance:* redundancy, latent defects, and calm methodical problem‑solving under failure — the
essence of reliability engineering.

**15 April — RMS Titanic sinks (1912)**
After striking an iceberg late on 14 April, the "practically unsinkable" ship sank in the early hours of
15 April; ~1,500 died. Contributing factors included steel and rivets that became brittle in cold water
and watertight bulkheads that didn't extend high enough.
*Relevance:* material behaviour at low temperature, design margins, and the danger of overconfidence —
"it can't fail" is where failures begin.

**16 April — Texas City disaster (1947)**
A fire aboard the SS Grandcamp detonated ~2,300 tons of ammonium nitrate fertiliser, triggering one of
the worst industrial accidents in US history (~581 dead) and a chain of secondary explosions.
*Relevance:* reactive‑chemical hazards, bulk storage and handling, and the scale of consequence when
an energetic material is underestimated.

**25 April — Structure of DNA published (1953)**
Watson and Crick's one‑page paper in *Nature* described the DNA double helix, drawing critically on
Rosalind Franklin's and Maurice Wilkins' X‑ray work. It launched molecular biology.
*Relevance:* the intellectual starting point of the entire biotechnology industry that Pure Transfer's
sector now serves.

**26 April — Chernobyl disaster (1986)**
A flawed reactor design plus a mishandled safety test caused an explosion and the worst nuclear release
in history. Operators had disabled safety systems and worked outside procedures.
*Relevance:* safety culture, the hazard of overriding safeguards, and how process deviations become
catastrophes.

**28 April — Aloha Airlines Flight 243 (1988)**
A large section of the fuselage roof tore away in flight, yet the crew landed the aircraft with one
fatality. The cause was widespread metal fatigue and corrosion in an ageing, high‑cycle airframe.
*Relevance:* fatigue, corrosion and the necessity of inspecting pressure boundaries throughout their
service life — not just when new.

### May

**14 May — Edward Jenner's first vaccination (1796)**
Jenner inoculated young James Phipps with cowpox and showed it protected against smallpox — the first
scientific demonstration of vaccination.
*Relevance:* the distant origin of vaccine manufacturing and biologics, and of prevention as a
deliberate engineering goal.

**16 May — Shewhart invents the control chart (1924)**
Walter Shewhart of Bell Telephone Laboratories wrote a roughly one‑page memo containing the first
statistical control chart, distinguishing normal ("common cause") variation from "special cause"
signals worth acting on.
*Relevance:* the birth of Statistical Process Control and modern quality management — knowing when to
adjust a process and when to leave it alone.

**20 May — The Metre Convention is signed (1875)**
Seventeen nations signed the Treaty of the Metre in Paris, creating the international system of
measurement and the bodies that maintain it — the root of today's SI units.
*Relevance:* metrology and traceability — every calibration, every validated instrument and every
"how do we know it's accurate?" ultimately traces back to agreements like this.

**20 May — ASME BPE first edition approved (1997)**
The ASME Bioprocessing Equipment standard was approved as an American National Standard, giving the
pharmaceutical and biotech industries a common rulebook for hygienic design: surface finishes,
materials, fittings, weld quality and inspection.
*Relevance:* this is squarely Pure Transfer's world — ASME BPE is the reference for high‑purity,
cleanable, contamination‑resistant process equipment. (Two strong events fall on 20 May.)

**31 May — Johnstown Flood (1889)**
The South Fork Dam failed above Johnstown, Pennsylvania, releasing ~20 million tons of water and killing
over 2,200 people. The dam had been poorly modified and inadequately maintained by its owners.
*Relevance:* deferred maintenance and quiet modification of critical infrastructure — small neglected
changes with enormous downstream consequences.

### June

**1 June — Flixborough disaster (1974)**
A temporary bypass pipe installed at a chemical plant in Lincolnshire, UK ruptured, releasing cyclohexane
that exploded and killed 28 people. The bypass had been fitted quickly without proper engineering
design or review.
*Relevance:* management of change — the single most transferable lesson for anyone modifying pipework.
A "temporary" unreviewed connection can level a plant.

**4 June — Ariane 5 Flight 501 explosion (1996)**
The maiden Ariane 5 rocket self‑destructed ~37 seconds after launch. Flight software reused from
Ariane 4 hit a numerical overflow in conditions the older rocket never saw, and was never re‑tested for
the new vehicle.
*Relevance:* revalidation on change — reused, "proven" components must be re‑qualified for their new
context. A validation lesson written in fireballs.

**25 June — Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act signed (1938)**
Passed in the wake of the 1937 Elixir Sulfanilamide disaster — where a sweet‑tasting solvent
(diethylene glycol, effectively antifreeze) killed ~100 people, many of them children — the Act
required proof of safety before a drug could be sold and vastly expanded FDA authority.
*Relevance:* excipient and solvent purity, toxicology, and the foundation of modern pharmaceutical
regulation. Standards exist because someone learned the hard way.

**30 June — Pure Food and Drug Act signed (1906)**
The US Pure Food and Drug Act (and Meat Inspection Act the same day) outlawed adulterated and
mislabelled food and drugs, and set the country on the path to what became the FDA. Public pressure
followed exposés of filthy production conditions.
*Relevance:* the origin of food‑ and drug‑purity regulation, and of the idea that manufacturers must
prove cleanliness rather than assert it.

### July

**1 July — Ignaz Semmelweis born (1818)**
Semmelweis showed in 1847 that doctors washing their hands in a chlorine solution dramatically cut
deadly childbed‑fever deaths — years before germ theory explained why. He was ignored and ridiculed in
his lifetime.
*Relevance:* the patron saint of prevention over reaction — a simple hygienic control, resisted by a
culture that didn't want to hear it. Contamination control is often unglamorous and always worth it.

**6 July — Piper Alpha disaster (1988)**
An explosion and fire destroyed the North Sea oil platform, killing 167 — the world's deadliest
offshore disaster. A key factor was a permit‑to‑work breakdown: a pump was restarted with a
safety‑critical valve missing, its removal not properly communicated.
*Relevance:* isolation, lockout/permit‑to‑work and communication at shift handover — the human systems
that keep fluid‑handling plants safe.

**10 July — Seveso disaster (1976)**
A runaway reaction at a chemical plant near Seveso, Italy released a cloud containing highly toxic
dioxin over a populated area. It led directly to the EU's "Seveso Directive" on major‑accident hazards.
*Relevance:* containment, reaction control and the regulatory frameworks that now govern hazardous
chemical processing across Europe.

**17 July — Hyatt Regency walkway collapse (1981)**
Two suspended walkways in a Kansas City hotel collapsed onto a crowded lobby, killing 114. A
seemingly minor connection‑detail change during construction doubled the load on a critical joint — and
no one re‑ran the numbers.
*Relevance:* a design change without re‑analysis, and engineering responsibility for the details of a
connection. Small drawing changes can be lethal; someone must own the check.

**20 July — Apollo 11 Moon landing (1969)**
Humans landed on the Moon and returned safely, the culmination of an immense systems‑engineering effort
built on redundancy, testing and disciplined risk management.
*Relevance:* the high‑water mark of reliability engineering — proof of what rigorous design,
verification and margins can achieve.

### August

**6 August — Alexander Fleming born (1881)**
The Scottish bacteriologist who would later discover penicillin, launching the antibiotic era.
*Relevance:* the human root of large‑scale sterile fermentation and antibiotic manufacturing.

**1 August — I‑35W Mississippi River bridge collapse (2007)**
A bridge in Minneapolis collapsed during rush hour, killing 13. Investigators found undersized gusset
plates — a decades‑old design error — overwhelmed as loads on the bridge grew over the years.
*Relevance:* latent design defects, load growth over a structure's life, and the limits of visual
inspection for finding a flaw that's been there since day one.

**13 August — Harry Brearley casts the first stainless steel (1913)**
In Sheffield, while seeking a harder alloy for gun barrels, Brearley produced a chromium steel (~12.8%
Cr) that stubbornly resisted rust and acid etching. He recognised it as "rustless steel."
*Relevance:* the single most important material in Pure Transfer's world. Every 316L tube, hygienic
fitting and polished vessel descends from this cast. Corrosion resistance = purity, cleanability and trust.

**29 August — Faraday demonstrates electromagnetic induction (1831)**
Michael Faraday showed that a changing magnetic field induces an electric current, the principle behind
generators, transformers and countless sensors.
*Relevance:* the foundation of the electricity, instrumentation and automation that run and monitor
every modern process plant.

### September

**8 September — John Snow and the Broad Street pump (1854)**
Snow persuaded authorities to remove the handle of a public water pump he had linked to a deadly London
cholera outbreak, in effect founding modern epidemiology — and proving contaminated water kills.
*Relevance:* water quality and contamination of a shared supply — the deep backstory to Purified Water
and Water‑for‑Injection systems, and to evidence‑based prevention.

**12 September — First integrated circuit demonstrated (1958)**
Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments demonstrated the first working integrated circuit, launching the
microelectronics age.
*Relevance:* the birth of semiconductor manufacturing — an industry utterly dependent on ultrapure
water, ultrapure chemicals and gases, and cleanrooms. One of Pure Transfer's core sectors.

**23 September — Mars Climate Orbiter lost (1999)**
NASA's orbiter was destroyed on arrival at Mars because one team used imperial units (pound‑force
seconds) and another used metric (newton‑seconds) for the same data. Nobody caught the mismatch.
*Relevance:* units, interface control and verification between teams — a spotless reminder that the
boring checks (does everyone agree on units and specs?) prevent expensive failures.

**28 September — Alexander Fleming discovers penicillin (1928) ⚠**
Returning to his lab, Fleming noticed a *Penicillium* mould had killed bacteria on a contaminated
culture plate — the observation that led to antibiotics. *(The commonly cited date is 28 September;
the exact day is historically fuzzy — worth flagging if you post it as a specific date.)*
*Relevance:* the origin of antibiotics and of large‑scale sterile fermentation, and a lesson that even
a contamination event, properly observed, can teach something.

### October

**4 October — Sputnik 1 launched (1957)**
The Soviet Union launched the first artificial satellite, opening the Space Age and the space race.
*Relevance:* the space and defence push of this era drove huge advances in contamination control and
cleanroom technology that later transformed pharmaceutical and semiconductor manufacturing.

**5 October — Johnson & Johnson recalls Tylenol nationwide (1982)**
After seven people in Chicago died from Tylenol capsules that had been laced with cyanide, J&J pulled
~31 million bottles from shelves. The response reshaped consumer safety and led to tamper‑evident
packaging and a federal anti‑tampering law.
*Relevance:* product integrity and packaging as the last line of defence — and a model of putting
safety and trust ahead of short‑term cost.

**10 October — Kefauver‑Harris Amendment signed (1962)**
Prompted by the thalidomide tragedy — kept out of the US largely by FDA reviewer Frances Kelsey — this
law required drugs to be proven *effective*, not just safe, and mandated Good Manufacturing Practice.
*Relevance:* a cornerstone of GMP and validation. The modern expectation that a process is documented,
controlled and proven traces to this amendment.

**14 October — W. Edwards Deming born (1900)**
The statistician and quality thinker whose ideas on statistical process control and continuous
improvement helped drive post‑war Japan's manufacturing transformation, and later the global quality
movement.
*Relevance:* quality as a system, not an inspection step; reducing variation; "prevention over
detection." The philosophical backbone of doing things properly.

**16 October — "Ether Day": first public surgical anaesthesia (1846)**
William Morton publicly demonstrated ether anaesthesia at Massachusetts General Hospital, transforming
surgery and, with later antisepsis, the whole trajectory of medicine.
*Relevance:* part of the arc from crude medicine to sterile, controlled clinical and pharmaceutical
practice.

### November

**7 November — Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse (1940)**
"Galloping Gertie" tore itself apart in a moderate wind four months after opening, as aeroelastic
flutter built up oscillations the designers never anticipated.
*Relevance:* resonance and dynamic loading — the hazard of designing only for static loads and
ignoring how a system behaves in motion. (Marie Curie was also born on 7 November, 1867 — pioneer of
radioactivity, materials science and measurement, if you prefer a scientist angle for the day.)

**8 November — Wilhelm Röntgen discovers X‑rays (1895)**
Röntgen detected a mysterious new radiation that could image the inside of solid objects, winning the
first Nobel Prize in Physics.
*Relevance:* the basis of radiographic non‑destructive testing — inspecting welds and pressure
boundaries for internal defects you could never see from outside.

**15 November — Intel 4004 microprocessor released (1971)**
Intel introduced the first commercially available single‑chip microprocessor, putting a computer's
core onto one chip.
*Relevance:* the seed of industrial automation, PLCs and process control — the digital nervous system
of every modern hygienic plant.

### December

**2–3 December — Bhopal disaster (1984)**
Water entered a storage tank of methyl isocyanate at a Union Carbide pesticide plant in India,
triggering a runaway reaction that released a toxic cloud over a sleeping city and killed thousands.
Multiple safety systems were out of service or undersized.
*Relevance:* the worst industrial chemical disaster in history — a devastating case in inherent safety,
containment, maintenance of safety systems and minimising hazardous inventory.

**15 December — First US Pharmacopeia published (1820)**
Eleven physicians meeting in the US Capitol published the first national Pharmacopeia, a uniform set of
quality standards for medicines — created because inconsistent, poor‑quality preparations were harming
patients.
*Relevance:* the ancestor of the pharmacopeial standards (USP monographs, WFI/Purified Water specs)
that still define purity and quality in pharmaceutical manufacturing.

**15 December — Silver Bridge collapse (1967)**
The Silver Bridge over the Ohio River collapsed suddenly in rush‑hour traffic, killing 46. A single
eyebar failed from a tiny stress‑corrosion crack — one component with no redundancy. It led directly to
national bridge inspection standards. *(Shares the date with the USP entry above — take your pick.)*
*Relevance:* stress‑corrosion cracking, single‑point failures, and the case for redundancy and
routine inspection of critical components.

**23 December — First working transistor (1947)**
Bardeen, Brattain and Shockley at Bell Labs demonstrated the first working transistor, the device that
made all modern electronics — and modern process control — possible.
*Relevance:* the root of the semiconductor industry and of the electronics behind every sensor,
controller and automated valve.

**27 December — Louis Pasteur born (1822)**
Pasteur established germ theory, developed pasteurisation, and disproved spontaneous generation with his
elegant swan‑neck flask experiments.
*Relevance:* the scientific foundation of sterilisation, hygienic processing and contamination control
across food, beverage and pharmaceutical manufacturing.

**28 December — Tay Bridge disaster (1879)**
The Tay Bridge in Scotland collapsed in a storm while a train was crossing, killing all aboard. The
inquiry blamed under‑design for wind loading plus poor cast‑iron quality and workmanship, made worse by
weak inspection and maintenance.
*Relevance:* design margins, materials quality control and honest inspection — a Victorian catalogue of
the failures that engineering standards later existed to prevent.

## Part 2 — Year‑Based Milestones (no single reliable calendar day)

Genuinely important, but the exact day either isn't well fixed or the development unfolded over time.
Slot these onto a relevant day, tie them to a related anniversary, or run them as evergreen posts. The
**year** is accurate; I've deliberately not invented a date.

### Standards, bodies & regulation

- **1662 — Boyle's Law published.** Robert Boyle quantified the pressure–volume relationship of a gas,
  an early cornerstone of the science behind pressure, vacuum and gas handling.
- **1679 — Denis Papin's steam digester.** Papin's "digester" (an early pressure cooker with a safety
  valve) demonstrated pressurised steam cooking — a direct conceptual ancestor of the autoclave and of
  steam sterilisation (SIP). Presented to the Royal Society.
- **1810 — Nicolas Appert's heat preservation.** Appert won a French prize for preserving food by
  heating it in sealed containers — the birth of thermal processing and canning, decades before Pasteur
  explained why it worked. Foundational to food & beverage safety.
- **1820 — USP founded** (see also the 15 December dated entry).
- **1856 — Bessemer process announced.** Henry Bessemer's process for mass‑producing steel cheaply
  (announced in an 1856 paper) made steel an everyday engineering material. Without cheap quality steel,
  none of the later stainless and hygienic developments happen.
- **1856 — First synthetic dye (mauveine).** 18‑year‑old William Perkin accidentally made the first
  aniline dye, effectively launching the modern synthetic chemical / fine‑chemicals industry.
- **1869 — Mendeleev's periodic table.** Presented to the Russian Chemical Society, it organised the
  elements and predicted undiscovered ones — the map underpinning materials science and chemistry.
  *(Old‑Style/New‑Style calendar issues make the exact day unreliable, hence listed by year.)*
- **1880 — ASME founded.** The American Society of Mechanical Engineers formed in New York; it went on
  to create the Boiler & Pressure Vessel Code, the B31 piping codes and, later, ASME BPE — the standards
  spine of process‑plant engineering.
- **1883 — Reynolds number.** Osborne Reynolds published his experiments distinguishing laminar from
  turbulent flow, giving engineers the dimensionless number still used to predict flow behaviour in
  every pipe and fitting. *(As direct to fluid transfer as it gets.)*
- **1884 — Chamberland filter & the autoclave.** Charles Chamberland (working with Pasteur) developed
  the pressurised‑steam autoclave and a bacteria‑proof porcelain filter — the roots of both steam
  sterilisation (SIP) and sterile filtration.
- **1909 — Haber ammonia synthesis.** Fritz Haber demonstrated fixing atmospheric nitrogen into
  ammonia; industrialised by Carl Bosch and BASF by 1913. High‑pressure catalytic reaction engineering
  at industrial scale — a landmark of chemical processing.
- **1914–1915 — First ASME Boiler & Pressure Vessel Code.** After decades of deadly boiler explosions,
  ASME issued the first BPVC, arguably the most consequential engineering standard ever written for
  pressure‑containing equipment.
- **1913/1930s — Stainless steel matures.** Following Brearley's 1913 cast, the family expanded
  (austenitic 18/8, then molybdenum‑bearing and low‑carbon "L" grades like 316L). Electropolishing of
  stainless was developed in the 1930s — the surface‑finishing route central to high‑purity cleanliness.
- **1928 — Saunders diaphragm valve invented.** P.K. Saunders, a mining engineer in South Africa,
  patented the weir‑type diaphragm valve to stop leaks in compressed‑air lines. It became a defining
  component of hygienic and high‑purity fluid handling — a flexible diaphragm isolating the process from
  the mechanism, with no crevices to trap product. *(Genuinely on‑brand for Pure Transfer.)*
- **1940s — HEPA filter developed.** High‑Efficiency Particulate Air filters emerged from WWII work
  (containing radioactive particles on the Manhattan Project) and later became the heart of cleanroom air
  handling.
- **1947 — ISO founded** (see also the 23 February dated entry).
- **1950 — Deming's quality lectures in Japan.** W. Edwards Deming taught statistical quality control to
  Japanese industry, helping ignite a manufacturing‑quality revolution; the Deming Prize followed in 1951.
- **1954 — Membrane filtration commercialised.** The founding of Millipore (1954) helped turn membrane
  filters into a practical industrial tool for microbiology and, later, sterile filtration.
- **1950s — Clean‑in‑Place (CIP) developed.** The dairy industry pioneered automated cleaning of closed
  process systems without dismantling them — the origin of CIP, now fundamental to hygienic and
  high‑purity processing.
- **1959–1960 — Reverse‑osmosis membranes.** Loeb and Sourirajan at UCLA developed the first practical
  asymmetric RO membranes, making large‑scale water purification feasible — a building block of modern
  Purified Water and WFI generation.
- **1962 — Laminar‑flow cleanroom.** Willis Whitfield at Sandia National Laboratories introduced the
  laminar‑flow cleanroom ("let the air be the janitor"), roughly 1,000× cleaner than the best rooms of
  the day. It transformed contamination control for both semiconductors and pharma.
- **1963 — First US GMP regulations / Federal Standard 209.** The Kefauver‑Harris mandate produced the
  first formal Good Manufacturing Practice regulations, and the US issued Federal Standard 209, the first
  cleanroom‑classification standard (later superseded by ISO 14644).
- **1960s — Orbital welding matures.** Automated orbital welding of tubing, developed for aerospace,
  became the standard for producing clean, repeatable, crevice‑free welds in semiconductor and pharma
  high‑purity tube systems.
- **1964 — LAL endotoxin test discovered.** Levin and Bang found that horseshoe‑crab blood cells clot in
  the presence of bacterial endotoxin; adopted into the US Pharmacopeia by 1980, it became the standard
  test protecting injectables and WFI from pyrogens.
- **1975 — Monoclonal antibodies.** Köhler and Milstein's hybridoma technique enabled monoclonal
  antibodies — now a huge class of biopharmaceuticals and a major driver of modern bioprocessing.
- **1976 — Genentech founded / recombinant DNA industrialised.** The founding of Genentech marked the
  start of the biotech industry; the first recombinant human insulin (Humulin) was approved in 1982 —
  the first drug made by genetically engineered organisms.
- **1978 — Major cGMP revision (US).** A significant overhaul of US current Good Manufacturing Practice
  regulations sharpened expectations for validation, documentation and process control.
- **1980 — ISPE founded.** The International Society for Pharmaceutical Engineering formed, becoming the
  professional home for pharma engineering — Baseline Guides, commissioning & qualification practice, and
  the "Baseline"/"GEP" language now used industry‑wide.
- **1985–1987 — Therac‑25 radiation overdoses.** Software race conditions and removed hardware
  interlocks in a radiation‑therapy machine led to fatal overdoses — a foundational case study in
  software safety, interlocks and the danger of trusting software you can't fully verify.
- **1986–1987 — Six Sigma born.** Bill Smith at Motorola coined "Six Sigma"; Motorola adopted it in
  1987. A rigorous, data‑driven framework for reducing defects and improving process capability.
- **1987 — ISO 9001 first published.** The international quality‑management‑system standard, now used by
  organisations worldwide as the baseline for "we do what we say, and we can prove it."
- **1989 — EHEDG founded.** The European Hygienic Engineering & Design Group formed to develop hygienic
  design guidelines for food (and increasingly pharmaceutical) equipment — cleanability, drainability and
  crevice‑free design. Directly relevant to hygienic fluid‑contact equipment.
